Artists across the UK are raising alarms over the unchecked use of artificial intelligence (AI) in creative industries, warning that their work is being exploited without compensation. Vocal artist Lily Dooner, 28, from Leominster, described her fear of AI replicating her voice through stolen recordings: 'What really scares me is the idea that one day I might be watching a film, and hear a voice created out of snippets of mine.' Her concerns align with broader anxieties among creators in Herefordshire, who argue that current copyright laws fail to protect them from AI models trained on their art. 'AI can give you something passable in seconds… for me to give you something it’s going to be hours of my time,' Dooner added, highlighting the disparity between AI’s efficiency and human creativity.  Historical tensions over AI’s impact on artists date back to 2023, when industry advocate Ed Newton Rex condemned AI firms for 'stealing artists’ work to build their products, then flood[ing] the market with knock-offs,' a practice he said undermines human musicians’ livelihoods. Tim Evans, artistic director of Herefordshire’s Powerhouse theatre, echoed these worries, stating, 'The genie is out of the bottle—these models cannot be built without hoovering up this vast amount of data.' While Evans acknowledged AI’s potential as a 'democratizing tool for creativity,' he stressed that artists must be compensated for their intellectual property. He cited his own use of AI to recreate his late brother’s voice for a play, a project that required ethical access to recordings—a contrast to the unregulated scraping of data by commercial models.  The debate has prompted calls for legal reform. Culture minister Peter Kyle pledged to address the issue, vowing to 'give them the rights they had in the analogue age… fit for the digital age.' Yet visual artist Rebecca Farkas criticized the government’s current approach, which places the onus on creators to opt out of AI training datasets. 'AI could look at my animations and copy them in exactly my style,' she warned, noting how prompts like 'make an animation like Rebecca Farkas' could automate creative theft. 'You’re helping one industry by taking away from another,' she said, underscoring the zero-sum conflict between AI developers and human artists.  As the industry grapples with balancing innovation and fairness, the absence of clear compensation frameworks leaves many creators in limbo. While some, like Evans, see AI as a collaborative tool, others fear it will erode the value of human talent. With legal and ethical questions unresolved, artists remain on high alert, demanding safeguards to protect their voices—both literal and metaphorical—from being commodified for free.
