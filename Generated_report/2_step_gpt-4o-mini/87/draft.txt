In a growing concern for artists, the rise of artificial intelligence (AI) has ignited fears that their unique skills may be exploited without proper compensation. Lily Dooner, a 28-year-old musician from Leominster, voiced her worries about AI technology potentially hijacking her distinctive sound. 'What really scares me is the idea that one day I might be watching a film, and hear a voice created out of snippets of mine,' she said. Dooner highlighted the speed at which AI can generate content, noting, 'AI can give you something passable in seconds… for me to give you something it's going to be hours of my time.' She also underscored the limitations of AI, stating, 'The difference is AI can't give you something incredible. I can.'nnAlongside Dooner, visual artist Rebecca Farkas expressed similar concerns, emphasizing the unfairness of a system that puts the onus on creators to opt out from their work being used to train AI. 'AI could look at my animations, and then it could copy it in exactly my style… you could put a prompt in to say 'make an animation like Rebecca Farkas',' she explained. This mirrors the historical tension within the creative community regarding copyright protections, as artists like Sir Elton John have challenged government policies on AI and copyright exemption matters.nnTim Evans, artistic director of Powerhouse theatre, further elaborated on the dilemma. Reflecting on the aftermath of his brother's death, Evans used AI to recreate his brother's voice for a play, yet acknowledged that the fundamental issue remains: 'These models cannot be built and will not be built without hoovering up this vast amount of data.' He believes that while AI can be a transformative tool, artists must be compensated fairly for their intellectual property.nnPublic discourse surrounding the implications of AI on creative industries has prompted actions from government officials. Peter Kyle, a political figure engaged in addressing these issues, stated, 'I am offering to look comprehensively at the challenges creatives have into the future,' emphasizing a need for rights that resonate with both the analogue and digital age. Despite the potential of AI to democratize creativity, artists in Herefordshire and beyond are calling for urgent reforms to protect their livelihoods as this technological landscape evolves.
