Artists across industries are voicing growing fears that artificial intelligence is leveraging their work without permission or compensation, potentially undermining their livelihoods. Lily Dooner, a vocalist from Leominster, embodies this concern, expressing anxiety that AI could replicate her unique voice. “What really scares me is the idea that one day I might be watching a film, and hear a voice created out of snippets of mine,” she said.nnThis apprehension stems from the way AI models are trained – by consuming massive datasets of existing creative work. The concern isn’t about AI creating original art, but rather its ability to mimic existing styles and voices, effectively cloning an artist’s talent. Dooner highlighted the time investment inherent in genuine artistry, stating, “AI can give you something passable in seconds… for me to give you something it’s going to be hours of my time.” Despite this, she maintains confidence in the enduring value of human creativity, asserting, “The difference is AI can’t give you something incredible. I can.”nnThese anxieties aren't isolated to vocalists. Visual artists, writers, and musicians all share similar concerns. The core issue revolves around copyright and fair use. Currently, many AI developers operate under the premise that “scraping” data from the internet for training purposes falls under fair use, a concept many artists dispute. Rebecca Farkas, a visual artist, fears AI could easily replicate her animation style based on readily available work online, stating, “AI could look at my animations, and then it could copy it in exactly my style… you could put a prompt in to say ‘make an animation like Rebecca Farkas’.”nnAdding to the complexity, the use of AI isn’t inherently negative. Tim Evans, artistic director of Powerhouse, points out the potential for AI to “be used as really powerful, democratizing tools for creativity,” and recounts using AI to recreate his brother’s voice after his death, a project that wouldn't have been possible otherwise. However, he also acknowledges, “The genie is out of the bottle - these models cannot be built and will not be built without hoovering up this vast amount of data,” underlining the fundamental problem of data sourcing.  As yet, the question of financial compensation for the usage of creative work remains unanswered.nnThe UK government is beginning to respond to these concerns. Peter Kyle has offered to conduct a comprehensive review of the challenges facing creatives in the age of AI, promising to “give them the rights that they have in the analogue age… fit for the digital age.” However, some, like Farkas, believe the current approach prioritizes the interests of AI developers. “At the moment the government favours putting the onus on creators to opt out of having their work scraped by AI,” she said, highlighting a perspective that many in the creative arts community are echoing – one where the burden of protecting their work falls disproportionately on them while the AI industry continues to profit from their creations.
