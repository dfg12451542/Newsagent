Cirencester History Festival is seeking recollections and artifacts from residents who remember the town’s Old Station in its prime, as the building will be temporarily reopened during the festival this October. The Grade-II listed station, designed by Isambard Kingdom Brunel, served the town for over a century before closing in 1965, and organizers hope to recreate its former glory for festival attendees.nnThe festival, running from October 25th to November 1st, features a diverse program of talks and activities centered on both local and global history. A key highlight will be the opportunity to tour the Old Station on the festival’s two Saturdays, an event dedicated to celebrating the “golden age of rail,” according to festival producer Jess Yarrow. nn“We know that there are lots of people out there who can remember the station in its heyday,” Ms. Yarrow stated.  She added that the festival aims to transform the currently “slightly sad, empty-looking building” into a vibrant snapshot of its past, complete with props like prams, benches, and luggage.  The festival is actively soliciting stories, photographs, and any memorabilia that residents might have tucked away.nnCurrently undergoing refurbishment by architect Jonathan Rixon, who intends to use the station as his offices, the building also holds historical significance beyond its function as a railway hub.  “The building holds many hidden treasures, including a boardroom where Brunel sought a meeting space outside London,” Rixon explained.  He emphasized the importance of preserving such industrial heritage, stating, “It’s important to protect our industrial heritage. I’m passionate about preserving this piece of history and sharing it with the town.”nnIn addition to guided tours led by Mr. Rixon, visitors can explore an audio listening project featuring stories of the station and a virtual reality experience of the station during the Victorian era, created in collaboration with students from Cirencester College. Organizers hope to evoke memories of the station’s role throughout the 20th century, from everyday commutes to its use during World War Two in welcoming evacuees and soldiers.
