In a significant development, thousands of South Korean medical students are set to return to their classes, marking the end of a 17-month-long boycott that stemmed from disagreements with government policies regarding medical education. The Korean Medical Association has confirmed this decision, stressing the importance of normalizing medical education and the healthcare system. Prime Minister Kim Min-Seok welcomed the end of the boycott, describing it as a 'big step forward' for both the students and the medical community. 'It's time to take a deeper look at the medical field, the Congress, and the government, so that citizens can help solve problems,' he stated.nnThe boycott, initiated in response to government proposals aimed at increasing medical school admissions, was driven by students' concerns that such measures would compromise the quality of their education. As junior doctors, who continue to strike over their own working conditions, some students had previously expressed deep dissatisfaction with the current state of medical training in South Korea. No clear timeline has been provided for the students' return to classes, but the Korean Medical Association has urged the government to restore the academic calendar and improve training conditions to facilitate their reintegration.nnOf note, the education ministry reported that a considerable number of students—8,305—will face grade retention, which means they will have to repeat the same academic year due to the extended boycott. The prolonged absence from classrooms has raised concerns about the future of medical training and healthcare delivery in South Korea, signifying a critical moment for the nation’s healthcare system. The decision to resume classes signals a willingness among medical students to collaborate with the government and parliament to address the systemic issues affecting the medical field.
