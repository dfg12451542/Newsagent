The revolutionizing impact of artificial intelligence on creative industries has sparked a growing concern among artists, who fear their talents might be exploited for insufficient or no compensation. Lily Dooner, a 28-year-old voice artist from Leominster, shared her fears with BBC Politics Midlands, emphasizing the risk of having her distinctive vocal style replicated by AI without consent. 'What really scares me is the idea that one day I might be watching a film, and hear a voice created out of snippets of mine,' she said. Such scenarios highlight the urgent call for enhanced copyright protections in the face of rapidly advancing technology.nnDooner, along with other artists, expresses this anxiety amidst the vast potential and rapid output capabilities of AI in creative sectors. 'AI can give you something passable in seconds... for me to give you something, it's going to be hours of my time,' she remarked. Despite the speed of AI, Dooner argues its reliance on mimicry stifles true creativity: 'The difference is AI can't give you something incredible. I can.' Her sentiments reflect a broader industry concern, with creators apprehensive about AI models automating the consumption and replication of creative labor.nnTim Evans, artistic director and CEO of the Herefordshire-based theater organization Powerhouse, acknowledges the transformative power of AI, having used it to recreate his late brother's voice in a theater production. While heralding AI's democratizing potential, Evans underscores the necessity for compensation to creators whose works contribute to AI models. 'The genie is out of the bottle - these models cannot be built and will not be built without hoovering up this vast amount of data,' he stated, highlighting the reality that AI's advancement is inextricably linked to the vast reservoir of human-created data.nnThe dialogue around AI and copyright is gaining momentum, with the government considering strategies to address the balance between innovation and rights protection. Despite these discussions, visual artist Rebecca Farkas articulates a pressing risk: 'AI could look at my animations, and then it could copy it in exactly my style... you could put a prompt in to say 'make an animation like Rebecca Farkas'.' This scenario encapsulates a major tension in the arts sector today, drawing criticism that technology's gains are threatening the future of traditional creative professions. As the debate unfolds, the creative community seeks assurances that their unique contributions are safeguarded and duly acknowledged in the age of artificial intelligence.
