Artists across various disciplines are voicing increasing fears that artificial intelligence (AI) is leveraging their creative talent without proper compensation or consent. The rapid advancement of AI voice and image generation tools has sparked a debate over copyright and intellectual property, leaving many creators concerned about the future of their livelihoods. Lily Dooner, a vocalist from Leominster, embodies this anxiety, stating, “What really scares me is the idea that one day I might be watching a film, and hear a voice created out of snippets of mine.”nnThis fear isn’t unfounded. AI models require massive datasets to function, and these datasets are often built upon pre-existing creative work – including voices, music, writing, and visual art – scraped from the internet. While proponents tout AI’s potential as a democratizing force in creativity, artists like Dooner argue the speed and ease with which AI can replicate styles undermines the value of human skill and effort. “AI can give you something passable in seconds… for me to give you something it’s going to be hours of my time,” she explained, but emphasizing, “The difference is AI can’t give you something incredible. I can.”nnTim Evans, artistic director of Powerhouse, a Herefordshire-based theatre organization, acknowledges the technological genie is already out of the bottle. “The genie is out of the bottle - these models cannot be built and will not be built without hoovering up this vast amount of data,” he stated. Evans himself has utilized AI to recreate his late brother's voice for a theatre production, demonstrating a potential positive application of the technology. However, he stresses the need for a fair system where artists are compensated when their work is used to train these models. nnCurrently, the onus largely falls on creators to actively opt-out of having their work used in AI training datasets, a process many find cumbersome and inadequate. Visual artist Rebecca Farkas expressed frustration with this approach, pointing out the ease with which AI can replicate an artist’s style based on existing work. “AI could look at my animations, and then it could copy it in exactly my style… you could put a prompt in to say ‘make an animation like Rebecca Farkas’,” she said.  This highlights a core concern: AI isn't just *assisting* creativity, it's potentially *replacing* it, and devaluing the original works in the process.nnResponding to these concerns, Peter Kyle, a government representative, has offered to comprehensively review the challenges facing creatives in the digital age. He stated he will aim to provide artists with “rights that they have in the analogue age… fit for the digital age,” while acknowledging the current situation leaves the question of compensation largely unanswered.  As the technology continues to evolve, finding a balance between innovation and protecting the livelihoods of artists remains a critical challenge.
